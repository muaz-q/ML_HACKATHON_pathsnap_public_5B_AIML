Part A — The problem and the data

A1. What problem are we solving?

A car records its GPS position about once per second. GPS is noisy: tall buildings reflect the signal (multipath), the receiver can lose the satellites for a while (dropouts), and every fix carries a few metres of random error. Plotted on a map, the dots wobble around the road, sometimes land on a parallel street, and sometimes jump.

Map-matching means answering, for every GPS dot, "which road segment was the car really on?", and then joining those answers into a route the car could physically have driven. That is PathSnap.

One-sentence summary of our solution. For every GPS fix we list the 12 nearest road segments (candidates). A machine-learning classifier scores how likely each candidate is the true one. A Hidden Markov Model then picks the single most likely sequence of segments with the Viterbi algorithm, using the road network to rule out impossible jumps.
"We split map-matching into a local question and a global question. The classifier answers the local one: given this fix and its neighbourhood, how likely is each nearby road? The HMM answers the global one: which chain of roads is consistent over the whole trip and actually connected on the map?"

A2. The four raw file types

Each trip is a folder <id>/ with four headerless, whitespace-separated text files:

FileColumnsMeaningKey detail
.tracklongitude, latitude, time (s)The GPS observations, one row per fixNominally 1 Hz, but there are gaps. Longitude comes first.
.nodeslongitude, latitudeRoad-network junctions and shape pointsThe row number (0-based) is the node id.
.arcsfrom_node, to_nodeDirected road segments (straight lines between two nodes)The row number is the arc id. A two-way street appears as two arcs, A→B and B→A.
.routearc_idThe reference ("ground-truth") route: the arcs driven, in orderNot one row per GPS fix. It has no timestamps.

How they relate: a GPS fix is a point; arcs are line segments whose endpoints are looked up in .nodes; the route is a chain of arc ids. Nothing links row i of the track to row i of anything else. That is exactly the warning in the dataset brief, and it is why Part B2 exists.

Surprise found during exploration: every trip ships its own local road network. Trip 1 is in Germany, trip 21 in Vietnam, trip 80 in Argentina. Node 5 in one trip has nothing to do with node 5 in another. So the model must never use ids or absolute coordinates as features: they would not transfer to a new trip (or a new country).

What to take from this table:

  • The files are clean at the format level. There are no NaNs, no impossible coordinates and no out-of-order timestamps. We still sort by time, drop duplicate timestamps and drop non-finite rows in pathsnap/data.py, so the code is safe on the unseen evaluator trips.
  • Sampling is mostly 1 Hz but not always. 22 of 80 training trips contain at least one gap longer than a minute; the longest gap is over 40 hours (trip 90). The HMM must handle gaps (Part E5).
  • The networks are disconnected in places. Almost every trip's network is a cut-out of a bigger map, so roads at the edge dangle and some pieces are not connected. A shortest-path search can therefore fail, and the HMM must survive that (Part E6).
  • Most fixes lie within about 5–8 m of their road, which is typical consumer-GPS error.

Unreliable fixes are excluded from training and from scoring, and we report how many there are. This is the "reference route limitations" point the brief asks teams to identify.

Part B — Preparing the learning problem

B1. Coordinates: why we project to metres

Longitude and latitude are angles. One degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator but only about 64 km at Darmstadt (50°N), so Euclidean distance in degrees is wrong, and wrong by a different amount in each country. We therefore project every trip into a local Azimuthal Equidistant (AEQD) projection centred on that trip's GPS centroid, using pyproj. Afterwards, x and y are in metres and ordinary geometry works.

  • Why AEQD rather than UTM? The trips cover the whole globe. AEQD centred on the trip needs no zone bookkeeping, is exact for distances from the centre, and its distortion is far below GPS noise (<0.1 %) at the scale of one trip (tens of km).
  • Coordinate order: the files store longitude first. pyproj is called with always_xy=True so lon=x and lat=y.
"Degrees are not metres, and the trips are on every continent, so each trip is projected into its own azimuthal-equidistant frame centred on the trip. Inside a trip, distances are then accurate to well under a percent, which is far below the GPS error."

B2. Turning the route into a label for every GPS fix

To train a classifier we need, for every fix, "the correct arc". The .route gives the correct arcs in order but not when each was driven. We recover the timing with a monotonic alignment by dynamic programming (pathsnap/labels.py), which is the same idea as Dynamic Time Warping.

  • Let fix t be assigned to route position j(t). A car never drives its own route backwards, so the positions must be non-decreasing: j(0) ≤ j(1) ≤ … .
  • Assigning fix t to route arc j costs the point-to-segment distance c[t,j].
  • We pick the assignment with the smallest total cost: 
    D[t, j] = c[t, j] + min over j' ≤ j of D[t-1, j']
    The inner "min over j' ≤ j" is a running (prefix) minimum, so the whole thing is O(T × R) and runs in under a second per trip. We store which j' achieved the minimum and walk backwards from the end to read off the assignment.
  • Route positions may be skipped, because a 5 m arc driven at 15 m/s may receive no fix at all.
Tiny worked example. Route = [arc 7, arc 9, arc 4]. Fixes f0, f1, f2, f3. Distances (m): 
        arc7  arc9  arc4
f0       3    40    90
f1       6     5    70
f2      30     4    20
f3      80    25     2
Nearest-arc alone would also work here, but suppose f2 were 3 m from arc 7 (a road crossing back). The monotonic rule forbids going back to arc 7 after arc 9, so f2 stays on arc 9. Result: f0→7, f1→9, f2→9, f3→4.

The label distance (fix to its aligned arc) is what gives us the 60 m reliability rule from Part A3.

"The route file isn't per-fix, so we derived per-fix labels by aligning the fixes to the route in order with dynamic programming, like DTW, under the constraint that the vehicle can't move backwards along its own route. Fixes more than 60 m from their aligned arc are where the reference doesn't cover the track, so we excluded them."

B3. Candidate generation

For a fix, the answer is almost always one of the few roads nearby. For each fix we therefore take the K = 12 nearest directed arcs as candidates (ArcIndex in pathsnap/features.py):

  1. Keep only the arcs inside the track's bounding box plus 400 m (some networks have 300 k arcs).
  2. Merge each two-way pair (A→B and B→A share the same geometry), sample a point every 8 m along each segment, and build a KD-tree over the samples.
  3. For each fix, fetch the 160 nearest samples, walk them in distance order collecting distinct segments, and expand each segment to its directed arcs until there are 12.

Candidate miss rate is the share of fixes whose reference arc is not among the 12 candidates. If the true answer is not in the candidate list, no model can pick it. On reliable fixes it is 0.89% in train and 0.08% in student-test, so the candidate set is not the bottleneck.

Because two-way roads produce both directions, a fix typically has about 6 physical roads × 2 directions. Telling the two directions apart is a big part of the job, which is why heading features matter (B4).

B4. Feature engineering: 23 features per (fix, candidate) pair

Each row of the training table is one (fix, candidate) pair, with target y = 1 if the candidate is the reference arc. That gives 2,168,454 training rows. None of the features uses absolute coordinates or ids, so the model works in any city.

"Each row is one fix paired with one nearby road. Besides raw distance we built relative-distance, heading-agreement and, most importantly, temporal-context features: how close the neighbouring fixes are to the same road. That is what lets a per-row classifier reject a parallel street that one noisy fix happens to touch."

Part C — Models

C1. The metrics, precisely

The classifier outputs a probability p for every (fix, candidate) row. The decision for a fix is its highest-probability candidate. Code: pathsnap/metrics.py.

MetricDefinitionWhy we report it
Observation-level Top-1 accuracy (primary)Share of reliable fixes whose argmax candidate is the reference arc. A candidate miss counts as wrong.This is the actual map-matching answer and what the evaluator's accuracy measures
Macro F1Every candidate row gets a predicted label: 1 if it is its fix's argmax, else 0. Compute F1 for class 1 and for class 0, then average them.Required by the brief; balances the rare positive class against the common negative class
Precision / recall (class 1)Of the arcs we picked, the share that are correct; of the correct arcs, the share we pickedBecause we pick exactly one per fix, both are ≈ Top-1. The tiny difference comes from candidate misses
Log lossBinary cross-entropy of the raw probabilitiesMeasures probability quality, which matters because the HMM uses the probabilities themselves, not just the argmax
Candidate miss rateShare of fixes whose reference arc is not among the candidatesAn upper bound on achievable accuracy
Route continuityShare of consecutive fix pairs whose predicted arcs are the same, or where the second is reachable on the directed graph within max(300 m, 3 × GPS step + 150 m)Does the prediction describe a drivable path, or does it teleport?
Route arc precision / recall / F1Compare the set of arcs in the reconstructed route with the reference route's setSequence-level quality
Length error(length of reference arcs missed + length of wrong arcs added) / reference length, as in Newson & Krumm (2009)Route quality weighted by distance; lower is better
Why Top-1 is the primary metric and not Macro F1: with 12 candidates per fix, about 11 of 12 rows are negatives, so even a mediocre model has a high F1 on class 0, which inflates Macro F1 (the nearest-road baseline already gets 0.7617 Macro F1 but only 56.45% Top-1). Top-1 asks the real question: did we pick the right road?

C2. Validation protocol: why we group by trip

  • GroupKFold(5) with trip as the group. All rows of a trip go into the same fold. Neighbouring fixes are almost identical, so a random row split would put near-copies in train and validation and give an inflated score. Grouping imitates the real situation: the evaluator's trips are trips we have never seen.
  • Every model is fitted on the same random sample of 30,000 whole fixes (≈ 360 k rows) from the training folds. That is fair across models and keeps slow learners tractable. Validation uses all rows of the held-out fold, except for KNN, whose prediction cost is O(train × validation): it is validated on a random 15,000-fix sample of each held-out fold (documented in the code).
  • After CV, each model is refitted on a 30,000-fix sample of all 80 training trips and scored on the 10 student-test trips.
  • Seeds are fixed (42). Training and inference times are recorded.

C3. Reference point: the non-learned baseline

"Pick the nearest arc" (rank-1 distance) gets Top-1 = 56.45% on student-test. That is low because the nearest arc is often the opposite direction of the right road (same geometry, distance tie) or a parallel or crossing road. Every learned model beats it by about 30 points.

C5. The ten models, one by one

For each model: the idea, why it fits this problem, strengths and limits as seen in our results, configuration, loss, results, and an over/underfitting check. The over/underfitting evidence is the gap between CV (unseen trips) and student-test, plus fold variance. A training-set score alone is meaningless and was not used for selection.

Model 1: LogisticRegression (Linear)

A. Idea. A linear model: score = w·x + b, squashed by the sigmoid into a probability. Features are standardised first.

Why suitable. A strong, fast, well-calibrated baseline. If the features are good, many candidates are already separable by a weighted sum of distance, heading agreement and context distance.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: fast, stable, gives decent probabilities (low log loss). Limits: only straight-line decision boundaries, so it cannot express interactions such as 'heading only matters when the car is moving' without hand-made interaction features.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). C = 1.0 (inverse L2 regularisation strength), lbfgs solver, max_iter 2000, StandardScaler. Objective / loss: Binary cross-entropy (log loss) + L2 penalty.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 86.73%, Macro F1 0.9275, log loss 0.0664, precision 0.8673, recall 0.8680, classifier-only route continuity 98.68%; fit 1.8 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 3.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 87.18% ± 1.51% across folds vs student-test 86.73% (difference -0.45 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 2: GaussianNB (Probabilistic)

A. Idea. Bayes' rule with the 'naive' assumption that, given the class, every feature is an independent normal distribution.

Why suitable. The simplest probabilistic model: a useful check of how much the features carry on their own.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: trains in a fraction of a second and needs no tuning. Limits: our features are strongly correlated (dist, dist_rank, ctx_mean_near all measure closeness), so the independence assumption counts the same evidence several times and makes the probabilities extremely over-confident. That is exactly why its log loss is roughly ten times worse than every other model's, even though its ranking (Top-1) is still reasonable.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). var_smoothing = 1e-9 (default). Objective / loss: None iterative: closed-form maximum-likelihood estimates of each class's mean and variance.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 84.18%, Macro F1 0.9136, log loss 0.7602, precision 0.8418, recall 0.8425, classifier-only route continuity 98.58%; fit 0.2 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 9.0 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 85.62% ± 1.57% across folds vs student-test 84.18% (difference -1.44 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable. Underfitting in the probabilistic sense: its log loss (0.760) is far worse than logistic regression's (0.066) on the same features.

Model 3: KNN (Instance-based)

A. Idea. To score a row, find the 50 most similar training rows (Euclidean distance on standardised features) and take a distance-weighted vote.

Why suitable. Instance-based: no assumptions about the shape of the boundary, and 'similar situations had similar answers' is plausible for map-matching.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: flexible and simple. Limits: the prediction cost grows with the training size (O(train × query)). Here it was by far the slowest model, thousands of times slower than LightGBM per fix, which made it impractical for live use. It is also sensitive to feature scaling and to irrelevant dimensions in 23-D space.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). k = 50, weights = distance, brute-force search, StandardScaler. Objective / loss: None (lazy learner, no training objective).

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 87.57%, Macro F1 0.9321, log loss 0.0748, precision 0.8757, recall 0.8764, classifier-only route continuity 98.17%; fit 0.3 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 8551.7 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 87.19% ± 1.87% across folds vs student-test 87.57% (difference +0.38 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 4: DecisionTree (Tree)

A. Idea. A single tree of if/else splits on features, chosen greedily to make the child nodes as pure as possible.

Why suitable. Captures non-linear rules and interactions directly ('if heading is valid and heading_cos < 0 then probably the wrong direction').

Strengths and limits. Strengths: interpretable, fast, non-linear. Limits: high variance. One tree overfits small quirks, which shows in the largest fold-to-fold spread of all the models. Probabilities are coarse (leaf frequencies).

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). max_depth = 12, min_samples_leaf = 20. Objective / loss: Gini impurity reduction at each split.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 89.52%, Macro F1 0.9428, log loss 0.0675, precision 0.8952, recall 0.8960, classifier-only route continuity 98.81%; fit 11.7 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 1.7 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.05% ± 2.05% across folds vs student-test 89.52% (difference +0.47 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable. Its fold std is the largest, which is the classic high-variance behaviour of a single tree.

Model 5: RandomForest (Ensemble (bagging))

A. Idea. Bagging: 200 deep trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample and considering a random subset of features at each split. The forest averages their votes.

Why suitable. Keeps the non-linearity of trees while averaging away most of a single tree's variance.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: robust, little tuning, good probabilities. Limits: large model files (~86 MB) and slower inference than boosting, and it does not focus on the hard cases the way boosting does.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 200, min_samples_leaf = 5, max_features = sqrt. Objective / loss: Gini impurity per tree; averaging of the trees.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 90.17%, Macro F1 0.9464, log loss 0.0461, precision 0.9017, recall 0.9025, classifier-only route continuity 98.91%; fit 66.6 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 54.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.86% ± 1.65% across folds vs student-test 90.17% (difference +0.31 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 6: ExtraTrees (Ensemble (bagging))

A. Idea. Like a random forest, but the split thresholds are drawn at random rather than optimised, which adds more randomness and lowers the variance further.

Why suitable. A cheaper, even more variance-reduced cousin of the random forest.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: fast to train, and the lowest CV standard deviation of the tree ensembles. Limits: random thresholds cost some accuracy here, and the model is the largest on disk (~177 MB).

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 200, min_samples_leaf = 5. Objective / loss: Gini impurity with random thresholds.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 89.60%, Macro F1 0.9433, log loss 0.0499, precision 0.8960, recall 0.8968, classifier-only route continuity 98.74%; fit 20.4 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 90.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.43% ± 1.51% across folds vs student-test 89.60% (difference +0.17 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 7: HistGradientBoosting (Boosting)

A. Idea. Gradient boosting: trees are added one after another, each fitting the errors (gradients of the log loss) of the ensemble so far. Feature values are binned into histograms for speed.

Why suitable. Boosting focuses on the hard, ambiguous candidates (parallel roads, opposite directions), exactly where accuracy is lost.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: accurate and fast, scikit-learn native. Limits: fewer tuning knobs than LightGBM or XGBoost, and the default early-stopping hold-out is a random row split.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). max_iter = 300, learning_rate = 0.1, max_leaf_nodes = 31. Objective / loss: Binary log loss, optimised by gradient boosting.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 90.55%, Macro F1 0.9485, log loss 0.0467, precision 0.9055, recall 0.9063, classifier-only route continuity 98.88%; fit 4.7 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 20.0 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.73% ± 1.64% across folds vs student-test 90.55% (difference +0.82 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 8: LightGBM (Boosting)

A. Idea. Histogram gradient boosting with leaf-wise tree growth: it always splits the leaf with the largest loss reduction, which gives deeper and more accurate trees for the same number of leaves.

Why suitable. The same boosting rationale, and the most efficient implementation. It also exposes rich regularisation (leaves, min child samples, sub-sampling, L1/L2) for the Bayesian search.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: the best CV and student-test Top-1, the best log loss, and fast inference on a small model file. Limits: leaf-wise growth can overfit small data, so it needs regularisation (min_child_samples, subsampling), and it is less interpretable than one tree.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 300, learning_rate = 0.05, num_leaves = 31 (untuned). Objective / loss: Binary log loss (gradient + Hessian boosting).

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 91.17%, Macro F1 0.9518, log loss 0.0427, precision 0.9117, recall 0.9124, classifier-only route continuity 99.07%; fit 5.1 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 61.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 90.05% ± 1.84% across folds vs student-test 91.17% (difference +1.12 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 9: XGBoost (Boosting)

A. Idea. Regularised gradient boosting with depth-wise trees and an explicit L2 penalty on the leaf weights, using the hist tree method.

Why suitable. The other industry-standard booster, included to check that LightGBM's lead is not a library accident.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: very close to LightGBM on every metric, with stable regularisation. Limits: slightly lower Top-1 and higher log loss than LightGBM here, and slower training.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 300, learning_rate = 0.1, max_depth = 6, tree_method = hist. Objective / loss: Binary log loss + L2 leaf regularisation.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 90.77%, Macro F1 0.9497, log loss 0.0437, precision 0.9077, recall 0.9085, classifier-only route continuity 98.99%; fit 7.5 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 20.7 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Training accuracy was never used.

Gradient-boosted trees were consistently best, and LightGBM had the best CV Top-1, the best student-test Top-1 and Macro F1, and the lowest log loss, so it gives the HMM the best-calibrated probabilities, at a few milliseconds per thousand fixes. Its fold-to-fold spread is similar to the other models', so the lead isn't one lucky fold."

Part D — Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation

D1. What "Bayesian optimisation" means here

Grid search tries every combination, and random search tries random ones. Bayesian optimisation learns from the trials so far where good settings are likely and samples there next. We used Optuna's TPE (Tree-structured Parzen Estimator):

  1. Run a few random "start-up" trials (8 for us).
  2. Split the trials so far into "good" (top ~15–25 %) and "bad".
  3. Fit two densities over the hyperparameters: l(x) from the good trials and g(x) from the bad ones.
  4. Propose the next x where l(x)/g(x) is largest, i.e. settings that look like past winners and unlike past losers. This ratio is equivalent to maximising Expected Improvement.
  5. Repeat. Each new result updates both densities.

Part E — HMM and Viterbi route reconstruction

E1. Why the classifier alone is not enough

The classifier scores each fix on its own (plus a small context window). It can choose arc A at time t and an unconnected arc C at t+1, i.e. a teleport. Within a trip it also never asks "can I actually drive from here to there?". On student-test the classifier-only argmax gives route continuity of 98.93%, but its stitched route has arc F1 of only 0.7752, because the brief detours it takes add many wrong arcs.

E2. The HMM in plain words

A Hidden Markov Model says: there is a hidden thing we cannot see (the true road) that changes over time following some rules (driving on a road network), and at each step we see a noisy observation of it (the GPS fix).

HMM pieceIn PathSnap
Hidden state at time tOne candidate arc for fix t, together with where the fix projects onto it (offset in metres). We keep the top K = 6 candidates by classifier probability per fix.
Emission P(observation | state)The classifier's probability, renormalised over the fix's K candidates: log e = w_e · log p_norm, with w_e = 2.0.
Transition P(state_t | state_t−1)Newson & Krumm (2009): the distance driven on the network between the two projected points should be close to the straight-line distance between the two GPS fixes:
log A = −| d_route − d_gps | / β, with β = 3.0 m (scaled by √Δt for longer steps).

How d_route is computed. Same arc, moving forward (or less than 15 m backwards, which is GPS jitter): the difference of the offsets. Different arcs: (rest of arc A) + (shortest path from A's end node to B's start node) + (offset on B). Shortest paths come from a bounded Dijkstra on the directed arc graph (search radius max(300 m, 3 × d_gps + 150 m)), cached per start node. Using the directed graph means one-way streets and wrong-way turns are respected automatically.

E3. The Viterbi algorithm

Viterbi finds the single most likely sequence of hidden states. It is dynamic programming over time:

δ_0(b)  = e_0(b)
δ_t(b)  = e_t(b) + max over a of [ δ_{t-1}(a) + log A(a → b) ]
ψ_t(b)  = the a that achieved the max     (back-pointer)
end:     b*_T = argmax δ_T ;  then b*_{t-1} = ψ_t(b*_t)  walking backwards

Everything is done in log space, so products of probabilities become sums and nothing underflows. The cost is O(T × K²) transitions; with K = 6 that is only 6² = 36 per step.

Mini example (2 candidates, 3 fixes). Log-emissions: t0 {A: −0.1, B: −2.3}, t1 {A: −1.2, B: −0.4}, t2 {A: −0.2, B: −1.8}. Transitions: staying on the same road costs 0, while A↔B (a parallel road, unconnected) costs −25.
δ1(A) = −1.2 + max(−0.1+0, −2.3−25) = −1.3; δ1(B) = −0.4 + max(−0.1−25, −2.3+0) = −2.7.
δ2(A) = −0.2 + max(−1.3, −2.7−25) = −1.5; δ2(B) = −1.8 + max(−1.3−25, −2.7) = −4.5.
Best path: A, A, A. At t1 the classifier alone preferred B (−0.4 > −1.2), but switching to an unconnected road and back is too expensive, so the HMM corrects the noisy fix.

E5. GPS gaps

  • Short irregular steps (Δt of a few seconds): β is multiplied by √Δt, so a longer step tolerates a larger mismatch between route and straight-line distance, and the Dijkstra radius grows with d_gps.
  • Big gaps (Δt > 60 s, or the fixes are more than 2 km apart): the chain is broken. The transition becomes uniform: decoding carries the best score so far and restarts from the emissions alone. Without this, a 40-hour gap would force a nonsense shortest path. Student-test triggered 1 such breaks.

E6. Disconnected candidates and missing paths

If no path from A to B exists within the search radius (dead-end network edge, disconnected component, or a one-way restriction), the transition gets a fixed penalty log A = −25.0 instead of −∞. Decoding therefore never dies: if every option is disconnected, the least-bad one is still chosen, and the emissions decide. There is always at least one candidate, because we take the 12 nearest arcs regardless of distance.

E7. From per-fix arcs to a continuous route

Viterbi gives one arc per fix. At 15 m/s, consecutive fixes can skip a short arc, so stitch_route() collapses repeats and fills any gap between two non-adjacent consecutive arcs with the shortest-path arcs between them. The result is a drivable chain, and this is what the UI draws and what the route-level metrics score.

F4. Leakage and integrity safeguards (be ready to point at the code)

  • Prediction loads the trip with with_route=False. The route is read only after prediction, inside the API, to compute the metrics. The reference geometry is never in the JSON response.
  • Features use no ids, no absolute coordinates and no route information. Labels (y) exist only in the training tables.
  • CV is grouped by trip. Bayesian search used only training trips. Decoder tuning used training trips (out-of-fold). Student-test was used for comparison only. Evaluator data was never touched.
  • There are no hard-coded predictions anywhere: the API computes everything live, and the timings are in the response and the logs.

F5. Honest limitations

  • The labels are derived: the per-fix label comes from our alignment of an untimed route, so a few fixes near junctions may be labelled with a neighbouring arc. Reported accuracies inherit that uncertainty. The reference itself also does not cover some tracks (trip 54).
  • Arcs are straight segments, because the files contain no curve geometry beyond the nodes.
  • Training the comparison models on a 30 k-fix sample was a fairness and time trade-off. The final model uses all rows.
  • The HMM uses K = 6 candidates. If the classifier ranks the true arc below 6th, the HMM cannot recover it.
  • Python Dijkstra is the slowest step on very long trips (still seconds per trip).

F6. Question bank (with answers)

Q1. What is map-matching?
Assigning each noisy GPS fix to the road segment the vehicle was actually on, and producing a connected route.
Q2. What does each raw file contain?
track: lon, lat, time per fix. nodes: lon, lat (row = node id). arcs: from-node, to-node (row = arc id, directed). route: the reference arc ids in driving order, with no timestamps.
Q3. Why can't you use the route file directly as labels?
It has one row per arc, not per fix, and no times. We aligned fixes to route arcs with a monotonic dynamic-programming alignment (like DTW) to get a per-fix label.
Q4. Why is the alignment monotonic?
A vehicle traverses its route in order. Forcing non-decreasing route positions stops a fix from being matched back to an earlier arc that happens to be nearby (e.g. at a crossing).
Q5. What are 'unreliable' fixes?
Fixes more than 60 m from their aligned reference arc. These are mostly parts of the track the reference route does not cover (trip 54 has ~88 % of such fixes). They are excluded from training and scoring and counted in the report.
Q6. Why project coordinates?
Degrees aren't metres and the scale differs with latitude. We use a per-trip azimuthal-equidistant projection, which is accurate in metres near the trip and works anywhere in the world.
Q7. How are candidates generated?
A KD-tree over points sampled every 8 m along every road near the track. Per fix we collect the nearest distinct segments, expanded to both directions, up to 12 directed arcs.
Q8. What is the candidate miss rate and why does it matter?
The share of fixes whose true arc isn't among the candidates. It caps the achievable accuracy. Ours is about 0.1 %, so the candidate stage isn't the bottleneck.
Q9. Which features matter most and why?
Temporal-context distances (how close the neighbouring fixes are to the same arc) and heading agreement. Context separates parallel roads, and heading separates the two directions of the same road.
Q10. Why not use latitude/longitude as features?
Every trip is in a different place with its own network. Absolute coordinates would let the model memorise places instead of learning matching behaviour, and they would not transfer to the evaluator's trips.
Q11. Why GroupKFold and not KFold?
Consecutive fixes are near-duplicates. A random split leaks the same trip into train and validation and inflates the scores. Grouping by trip simulates unseen trips.
Q12. What's your primary metric?
Observation-level Top-1 accuracy, i.e. whether the argmax candidate is the reference arc, because that is the decision the system makes. Macro F1 is inflated by the many easy negatives; log loss is secondary because the HMM uses the probabilities.
Q13. Why does nearest-road do so badly?
Two-way roads give two arcs with identical geometry, so 'nearest' can't tell the direction, and parallel or crossing roads are often equally close. Heading and context features fix this.
Q14. Why LightGBM?
Best CV Top-1, best student-test Top-1 and Macro F1, best log loss (best-calibrated emissions for the HMM), fast inference, small model. XGBoost was a close second.
Q15. Did any model overfit?
Student-test scores fall within about one fold-std of the CV scores for every model, so none generalised badly. The single decision tree shows the highest fold variance. GaussianNB has badly over-confident probabilities (huge log loss) because of its independence assumption.
Q16. What is Bayesian optimisation and how is it different from grid search?
It builds a model of which hyperparameters gave good scores and proposes the next trial where improvement is likely. We used Optuna's TPE, which compares densities of good versus bad trials. Grid and random search ignore past results.
Q17. What did you optimise and on what data?
Mean CV Top-1 over GroupKFold(5) on the 80 training trips only. Student-test was used once, afterwards, to compare tuned and untuned.
Q18. What are the hidden states of your HMM?
For each fix, its top-K candidate arcs (K from hmm_config.json), each with the fix's projected offset along the arc.
Q19. How are emissions computed?
The classifier probabilities renormalised over the fix's K candidates, in log space, times a weight w_e.
Q20. How are transitions computed?
exp(−|d_route − d_gps| / β): network shortest-path distance between the consecutive projected points versus the straight-line GPS distance (Newson & Krumm). The shortest paths come from a bounded Dijkstra on the directed graph.
Q21. Write the Viterbi recurrence.
δ_t(b) = e_t(b) + max_a[δ_{t−1}(a) + log A(a,b)], with a back-pointer ψ_t(b) = argmax. Backtrack from argmax δ_T.
Q22. How do you handle GPS gaps?
β scales with √Δt and the Dijkstra radius grows with the GPS distance. If Δt > 60 s or the gap is over 2 km, the chain breaks: uniform transition, and decoding restarts from the emissions.
Q23. What if no path exists between candidates?
That transition gets a finite penalty (−jump_penalty) instead of −∞, so Viterbi always returns a path and the emissions decide.
Q24. Did the HMM help?
Yes: it raised Top-1 and sharply raised route-level arc F1 and continuity (see Part E8). Where the classifier is already right, the HMM changes little; it fixes isolated wrong picks and teleports.
Q25. When did the HMM not help?
Where the classifier is confidently wrong over a long stretch (e.g. a long parallel service road), the HMM follows the wrong road consistently. And when the true arc is outside the top K, it cannot recover it.
Q26. How did you tune the decoder without leakage?
On training trips, using out-of-fold classifier probabilities (each trip scored by a fold model that never saw it). The evaluator data was never used.
Q27. How do you guarantee the ground truth doesn't reach the model or UI?
The API loads the trip with with_route=False, predicts, and only then reads .route to score. The response contains metrics but never the reference arcs.
Q28. What does the API return?
GPS fixes, the predicted (HMM) route polylines, the classifier-only route, the predicted arc per fix, nearby road network, server-side metrics and timings.
Q29. How long does a prediction take?
A few seconds per trip on a laptop (features, then classifier, then HMM). The timings are shown in the UI and the logs.
Q30. What is route continuity?
The share of consecutive fix pairs whose predicted arcs are the same, or where the second is reachable on the directed graph within a plausible driving distance. 100 % means no teleports.
Q31. Why is classifier-only continuity already high?
Most consecutive fixes stay on the same arc. The real damage shows at route level: a single wrong pick inserts a detour, which is why its route arc F1 is much lower.
Q32. What is log loss, intuitively?
The average 'surprise' of the true labels under the model's probabilities. Being confidently wrong is punished heavily. Lower is better.
Q33. How would you improve the system?
Curved road geometry, learning the transition model (e.g. turn-angle features), a structured/CRF-style joint model, a faster compiled Dijkstra, and more careful labels near junctions.
Q34. Why K=12 candidates but only K_hmm in the HMM?
12 keeps the miss rate near zero for training. The HMM's cost grows with K², and the classifier almost always ranks the true arc within its top few.
Q35. Why is the baseline LightGBM + HMM higher than the tuned LightGBM + HMM on student-test?
Bayesian tuning optimised the classifier's own CV Top-1, not the combined classifier+HMM. The tuned model is more confident, so the HMM can overrule fewer of its mistakes. We didn't re-select based on student-test, because that would overfit to those 10 trips. The fix is to tune both together on out-of-fold training trips.
Q36. How did it do on the evaluator's hidden trips?
We ran the frozen system on them without changing anything: 95.3% fix-level Top-1 with the HMM (92.1% without), mean route arc F1 0.956, at most 2.5 s per trip. That is slightly better than on our student-test trips, so it generalises. Trip 15 has a long route error only because the car drove the same loop about six times and the reference lists each road once.
Q37. Is the reference route perfect ground truth?
No. It is untimed, it doesn't always cover the whole track, and our per-fix labels come from an alignment. We excluded unreliable fixes and treat accuracies as estimates.
