Team5B AIML

Repositoryhttps://github.com/muaz-q/ML_HACKATHON_pathsnap_public_5B_AIML

Summary. For each GPS fix we generate the 12 nearest directed road arcs as candidates and compute 23 location-independent features per (fix, candidate) pair. A classifier estimates P(candidate = reference arc), and an HMM decoded with Viterbi turns these into a connected route using shortest-path distances on the directed road graph. Ten models were compared with trip-grouped 5-fold CV. LightGBM was selected and tuned with Bayesian optimisation (Optuna TPE). On the 10 student-test trips the final system reaches 93.68% observation-level Top-1 accuracy (classifier only: 91.00%) and route arc F1 0.9400 (classifier only: 0.7752). It is deployed as a FastAPI service (Laptop 1) with a Leaflet UI (Laptop 2).

2. Dataset Understanding and Exploratory Data Analysis

Partitions: all EDA uses train (80 trips) and student_test (10 trips). Evaluator trips were not available and were never used.

2.1 How did we preprocess the data?

  1. Loading and cleaning (pathsnap/data.py): read the four headerless files per trip; drop non-finite track rows, sort by timestamp, drop duplicate timestamps. The checks found 0 NaN rows, 0 invalid coordinates and 0 non-increasing timestamps in both partitions, but the steps are kept for unseen data.
  2. Projection: lon/lat are angular and the trips span every continent, so each trip is projected into a local azimuthal-equidistant frame (metres) centred on its GPS centroid (pyproj, always_xy because the files are lon-first). Distortion is <0.1 % at trip scale.
  3. Per-fix reference labels (pathsnap/labels.py): .route is an untimed arc sequence, not one row per fix. Each fix is assigned a route position by a monotonic dynamic-programming alignment minimising total point-to-arc distance, with D[t,j] = c[t,j] + minj'≤j D[t−1,j'].
  4. Reliability filter: fixes farther than 60 m from their aligned arc (22,345 train fixes, 10.7%; 25 in student-test) occur where the track extends beyond the reference route (e.g. trip 54: 88% of its fixes). They are excluded from training and scoring.
  5. Candidates (pathsnap/features.py): a KD-tree over points sampled every 8 m along road segments near the track; the 12 nearest directed arcs per fix. Candidate miss rate on reliable fixes: 0.89% (train), 0.08% (student-test).

Observed patterns. (i) Every trip carries its own local network, and the trips are worldwide, so ids and absolute coordinates cannot be features. 3. Model Experimentation and Comparison

Protocol. The task is binary classification of (fix, candidate) rows. Evaluation uses GroupKFold(5) by trip on the 80 training trips. Every model is fitted on the same 30,000-fix sample from the training folds; validation uses the full held-out fold (KNN: a 15,000-fix sample because of its O(train × query) cost). Each model is then refitted on a 30,000-fix sample of all training trips and scored on student-test. Seed 42. Primary metric: observation-level Top-1 accuracy (the argmax candidate is the reference arc), because it is the system's actual decision. Macro F1 uses the argmax decision per row (1 for the fix's argmax candidate, else 0). Log loss is computed on the raw probabilities. Route continuity is the share of consecutive fix pairs whose arcs are the same or reachable on the directed graph within max(300 m, 3 × step + 150 m). Non-learned reference (nearest arc): Top-1 56.45%, Macro F1 0.7617.

3.2 Per-model analysis

Model 1: LogisticRegression (Linear)

A. Idea. A linear model: score = w·x + b, squashed by the sigmoid into a probability. Features are standardised first.

Why suitable. A strong, fast, well-calibrated baseline. If the features are good, many candidates are already separable by a weighted sum of distance, heading agreement and context distance.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: fast, stable, gives decent probabilities (low log loss). Limits: only straight-line decision boundaries, so it cannot express interactions such as 'heading only matters when the car is moving' without hand-made interaction features.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). C = 1.0 (inverse L2 regularisation strength), lbfgs solver, max_iter 2000, StandardScaler. Objective / loss: Binary cross-entropy (log loss) + L2 penalty.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 86.73%, Macro F1 0.9275, log loss 0.0664, precision 0.8673, recall 0.8680, classifier-only route continuity 98.68%; fit 1.8 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 3.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 87.18% ± 1.51% across folds vs student-test 86.73% (difference -0.45 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 2: GaussianNB (Probabilistic)

A. Idea. Bayes' rule with the 'naive' assumption that, given the class, every feature is an independent normal distribution.

Why suitable. The simplest probabilistic model: a useful check of how much the features carry on their own.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: trains in a fraction of a second and needs no tuning. Limits: our features are strongly correlated (dist, dist_rank, ctx_mean_near all measure closeness), so the independence assumption counts the same evidence several times and makes the probabilities extremely over-confident. That is exactly why its log loss is roughly ten times worse than every other model's, even though its ranking (Top-1) is still reasonable.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). var_smoothing = 1e-9 (default). Objective / loss: None iterative: closed-form maximum-likelihood estimates of each class's mean and variance.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 84.18%, Macro F1 0.9136, log loss 0.7602, precision 0.8418, recall 0.8425, classifier-only route continuity 98.58%; fit 0.2 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 9.0 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 85.62% ± 1.57% across folds vs student-test 84.18% (difference -1.44 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable. Underfitting in the probabilistic sense: its log loss (0.760) is far worse than logistic regression's (0.066) on the same features.

Model 3: KNN (Instance-based)

A. Idea. To score a row, find the 50 most similar training rows (Euclidean distance on standardised features) and take a distance-weighted vote.

Why suitable. Instance-based: no assumptions about the shape of the boundary, and 'similar situations had similar answers' is plausible for map-matching.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: flexible and simple. Limits: the prediction cost grows with the training size (O(train × query)). Here it was by far the slowest model, thousands of times slower than LightGBM per fix, which made it impractical for live use. It is also sensitive to feature scaling and to irrelevant dimensions in 23-D space.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). k = 50, weights = distance, brute-force search, StandardScaler. Objective / loss: None (lazy learner, no training objective).

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 87.57%, Macro F1 0.9321, log loss 0.0748, precision 0.8757, recall 0.8764, classifier-only route continuity 98.17%; fit 0.3 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 8551.7 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 87.19% ± 1.87% across folds vs student-test 87.57% (difference +0.38 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 4: DecisionTree (Tree)

A. Idea. A single tree of if/else splits on features, chosen greedily to make the child nodes as pure as possible.

Why suitable. Captures non-linear rules and interactions directly ('if heading is valid and heading_cos < 0 then probably the wrong direction').

Strengths and limits. Strengths: interpretable, fast, non-linear. Limits: high variance. One tree overfits small quirks, which shows in the largest fold-to-fold spread of all the models. Probabilities are coarse (leaf frequencies).

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). max_depth = 12, min_samples_leaf = 20. Objective / loss: Gini impurity reduction at each split.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 89.52%, Macro F1 0.9428, log loss 0.0675, precision 0.8952, recall 0.8960, classifier-only route continuity 98.81%; fit 11.7 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 1.7 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.05% ± 2.05% across folds vs student-test 89.52% (difference +0.47 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable. Its fold std is the largest, which is the classic high-variance behaviour of a single tree.

Model 5: RandomForest (Ensemble (bagging))

A. Idea. Bagging: 200 deep trees, each trained on a bootstrap sample and considering a random subset of features at each split. The forest averages their votes.

Why suitable. Keeps the non-linearity of trees while averaging away most of a single tree's variance.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: robust, little tuning, good probabilities. Limits: large model files (~86 MB) and slower inference than boosting, and it does not focus on the hard cases the way boosting does.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 200, min_samples_leaf = 5, max_features = sqrt. Objective / loss: Gini impurity per tree; averaging of the trees.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 90.17%, Macro F1 0.9464, log loss 0.0461, precision 0.9017, recall 0.9025, classifier-only route continuity 98.91%; fit 66.6 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 54.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.86% ± 1.65% across folds vs student-test 90.17% (difference +0.31 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 6: ExtraTrees (Ensemble (bagging))

A. Idea. Like a random forest, but the split thresholds are drawn at random rather than optimised, which adds more randomness and lowers the variance further.

Why suitable. A cheaper, even more variance-reduced cousin of the random forest.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: fast to train, and the lowest CV standard deviation of the tree ensembles. Limits: random thresholds cost some accuracy here, and the model is the largest on disk (~177 MB).

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 200, min_samples_leaf = 5. Objective / loss: Gini impurity with random thresholds.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 89.60%, Macro F1 0.9433, log loss 0.0499, precision 0.8960, recall 0.8968, classifier-only route continuity 98.74%; fit 20.4 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 90.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.43% ± 1.51% across folds vs student-test 89.60% (difference +0.17 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 7: HistGradientBoosting (Boosting)

A. Idea. Gradient boosting: trees are added one after another, each fitting the errors (gradients of the log loss) of the ensemble so far. Feature values are binned into histograms for speed.

Why suitable. Boosting focuses on the hard, ambiguous candidates (parallel roads, opposite directions), exactly where accuracy is lost.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: accurate and fast, scikit-learn native. Limits: fewer tuning knobs than LightGBM or XGBoost, and the default early-stopping hold-out is a random row split.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). max_iter = 300, learning_rate = 0.1, max_leaf_nodes = 31. Objective / loss: Binary log loss, optimised by gradient boosting.

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 90.55%, Macro F1 0.9485, log loss 0.0467, precision 0.9055, recall 0.9063, classifier-only route continuity 98.88%; fit 4.7 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 20.0 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 89.73% ± 1.64% across folds vs student-test 90.55% (difference +0.82 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 8: LightGBM (Boosting)

A. Idea. Histogram gradient boosting with leaf-wise tree growth: it always splits the leaf with the largest loss reduction, which gives deeper and more accurate trees for the same number of leaves.

Why suitable. The same boosting rationale, and the most efficient implementation. It also exposes rich regularisation (leaves, min child samples, sub-sampling, L1/L2) for the Bayesian search.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: the best CV and student-test Top-1, the best log loss, and fast inference on a small model file. Limits: leaf-wise growth can overfit small data, so it needs regularisation (min_child_samples, subsampling), and it is less interpretable than one tree.

B. Hyperparameters (initial values). n_estimators = 300, learning_rate = 0.05, num_leaves = 31 (untuned). Objective / loss: Binary log loss (gradient + Hessian boosting).

C. Results (student-test). Top-1 91.17%, Macro F1 0.9518, log loss 0.0427, precision 0.9117, recall 0.9124, classifier-only route continuity 99.07%; fit 5.1 s on the 30 k-fix sample, inference 61.3 ms per 1,000 fixes.

Over/underfitting. CV Top-1 90.05% ± 1.84% across folds vs student-test 91.17% (difference +1.12 points). The student-test score lies within the fold-to-fold variation of the CV score, so there is no sign of overfitting to the training trips: performance on unseen trips is stable.

Model 9: XGBoost (Boosting)

A. Idea. Regularised gradient boosting with depth-wise trees and an explicit L2 penalty on the leaf weights, using the hist tree method.

Why suitable. The other industry-standard booster, included to check that LightGBM's lead is not a library accident.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: very close to LightGBM on every metric, with stable regularisation. Limits: slightly lower Top-1 and higher log loss than LightGBM here, and slower training.

Model 10: MLP (Neural network)

A. Idea. A feed-forward neural network: 23 inputs → 64 ReLU → 32 ReLU → 1 sigmoid output, trained with Adam.

Strengths and limits. Strengths: competitive accuracy and fast inference. Limits: needs scaling, early stopping and more data to beat trees on tabular features. Tabular data with heterogeneous features usually favours tree ensembles, as it does here.

3.4 Model selection

Selected: LightGBM. It has the highest CV Top-1 (90.05%), the highest student-test Top-1 (91.17%) and Macro F1 (0.9518), and the lowest student-test log loss (0.0427). 5. HMM and Viterbi Route Reconstruction

Q1. Hidden states: for each fix, its top K = 6 candidate arcs by classifier probability, each with the fix's projected offset along the arc.

Q2. Emissions: log et(a) = we · log p̂(a | t), where p̂ is the classifier probability renormalised over the K candidates; we = 2.0.

Q3. Transitions: log A(a→b) = −|droute − dgps| / β, β = 3.0 m × max(1, √Δt) (Newson & Krumm). droute = remaining length of a + shortest path from head(a) to tail(b) + offset on b, or the offset difference on the same arc (backward moves <15 m are treated as jitter). Shortest paths come from a bounded Dijkstra (radius max(300 m, 3·dgps+150 m)) on the directed graph, cached per source node. Viterbi: δt(b) = et(b) + maxa[δt−1(a) + log A(a,b)], with back-pointers.

Q4. Missing candidates / large gaps: every fix has at least one candidate (the nearest arcs are taken regardless of distance). If Δt > 60 s or dgps > 2 km, the chain is broken: a uniform transition, restarting from the best previous score (1 breaks on student-test).

Q5. Disconnected segments: an unreachable pair gets a finite penalty log A = −25.0 rather than −∞, so decoding never fails, and the decoded per-fix arcs are stitched into a continuous route by inserting shortest-path arcs where consecutive arcs are not adjacent.

Decoder tuning: a grid over β ∈ {3, 6, 10, 20}, we ∈ {0.5, 1, 2}, K ∈ {3, 4, 6, 8} on 24 training trips using out-of-fold probabilities. Chosen: β = 3.0 m, w_e = 2.0, K = 6. Across the β × w_e grid, HMM Top-1 on the training trips ranged from 90.47% to 91.14%, always above the classifier-only 88.69%, so the improvement does not hinge on a lucky setting.

An honest observation: the baseline LightGBM reaches 94.52% after the HMM, higher than the deployed tuned model's 93.68%, although the tuned model is better on the objective it was tuned for (CV Top-1). Tuning optimised the classifier alone, not the classifier+HMM combination. Our reading is that the tuned model, fitted on all rows, is more confident, so the HMM can overrule fewer of its mistakes. We did not switch models on the strength of this student-test number, because choosing by student-test result would overfit our selection to those 10 trips. Tuning the classifier and decoder jointly, with HMM Top-1 on out-of-fold trips as the objective, is the natural next step.

5.x Evaluator hold-out trips (released on review day)

On review day the evaluator's 10 held-out trips were released. We ran the frozen system on them (tuned LightGBM plus the HMM configuration chosen on training data; nothing was retrained or retuned) with scripts/08_eval_holdout.py, which predicts each trip without its route and only then scores it. No trip ID overlaps our training or student-test trips. Over 19,674 fixes, fix-level Top-1 was 92.08% for the classifier alone and 95.31% with the HMM; mean route arc F1 rose from 0.7931 to 0.9558, continuity reached 100.00%, and each trip took at most 2.5 s (the largest network has 107,451 arcs). These results are slightly better than on student-test (93.68%), which suggests the system was not overfitted to the trips we evaluated on.

7. API, UI and Deployment

Laptop 1 runs uvicorn api.main:app --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000 with PATHSNAP_EVAL_DIR pointing at the evaluation trips. Endpoints: GET /trips, POST /predict {trip_id}, GET /health. The API loads the trip without its route, runs features → LightGBM → HMM → stitching, then reads the reference only to compute the metrics server-side. The reference is never returned. Laptop 2 serves ui/index.html (Leaflet), which lists the trips, sends the selected id, and draws the GPS, network and predicted routes with the metrics and timings. Every request is logged on Laptop 1 (REQUEST / RESULT lines).
